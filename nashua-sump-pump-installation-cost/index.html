Sump Pump Installation Cost in Nashua, NH Homes

The cost of a sump pump installation depends mostly on whether a pit already exists, the pump type and horsepower, the discharge route and whether you add a battery backup and an alarm. Replacing a pump in an existing pit costs far less than cutting a new pit in a slab. Ask for a written quote that shows the pump, the backup, the discharge work and the labor separately.

By the Nashua Pro Plumbing team. Published October 20, 2026. Updated November 16, 2026.

What Changes the Price

Existing pit or new pitA pit already in the floor is the cheapest case. A new pit means cutting concrete, digging, setting a liner and patching.
Pump typeSubmersible pumps sit in the water and run quieter. Pedestal pumps sit above and are easier to service but louder.
Pump sizeHorsepower and flow rating should match the inflow, not just the budget.
DischargeA short run through the rim joist is simple. A long run to daylight takes more pipe and trenching.
Battery backupA second pump with its own battery protects you during outages and adds to the price.
Alarm and extrasA high water alarm, a check valve and a sealed lid add small amounts.

We do not print dollar figures because they change, vary by house and cannot be quoted without seeing the pit. A good installer gives you a written price for each line item.

New Pit, Replacement or Backup Only

Replacing a pump in an existing pit

  • Lowest cost and quickest
  • Usually done in a couple of hours
  • We check the check valve and discharge too

Cutting a new pit

  • Higher cost, more labor
  • Needs a liner, gravel and concrete patching
  • Best when you do not have a pit or the pit is poorly located

Adding a battery backup to a working pump sits in between. It is often a smart upgrade for a basement that has flooded before. See our sump pump page for the services we offer.

What a Good Installation Includes

  • ✓A pump sized for the inflow and the lift.
  • ✓A check valve so discharge does not drain back.
  • ✓A float switch that has room to move, not pinned against the pit wall.
  • ✓A discharge line that exits away from the foundation and does not freeze.
  • ✓A sealed lid where radon or humidity is a concern.
  • ✓A battery backup or water-powered backup where it fits.
  • ✓A walk through of how to test it.

Local Note: Where Sump Water Can Go

In Nashua, which is on municipal sewer, sump water must not be tied to the sanitary sewer. Ask the City of Nashua Department of Building Safety where discharge is allowed at your address. In Hollis, where homes use private septic, never run a sump pump into the septic system. It overloads the tank and the leach field.

Spring snowmelt and heavy rain raise groundwater in low parts of Nashua and near the rivers and Salmon Brook, and many basements see the pump run in those weeks. If your pump runs often, the price of a backup is small next to a flooded basement. We also serve Merrimack and Hudson.

Warning Signs You Should Replace the Pump Soon

A pump that runs constantly, short cycles, makes grinding noise or leaves water in the pit is on borrowed time. A float that sticks is a cheap fix, but a motor that overheats is not. If your pump is old and your basement has a history of water, replace it before the next storm rather than during it.

Sizing a Pump Without Overspending

Bigger is not always better. A pump that is too large short cycles, meaning it turns on and off quickly, which wears the motor and the switch. A pump that is too small runs constantly and cannot keep up in a storm. The right size depends on how fast water enters the pit and how high the pump must lift it, called the head.

We look at the pit size, the discharge height and how often the pump has run in the past. A pit that fills slowly is well served by a modest pump. A pit that fills in minutes during a storm needs more capacity and often a backup. Ask the installer to explain the sizing in plain terms.

Costs That Come Later

  • ✓Electricity, which is small for a pump that runs only during storms.
  • ✓Battery replacement for a backup system every few years.
  • ✓Testing and cleaning the pit once a year.
  • ✓Pump replacement when the motor wears out.
  • ✓Drainage fixes outside the house that reduce how often the pump runs.

A pump is an early warning as well. If it runs more than before, something has changed, such as a clogged downspout drain, a high water table or a failing pit. Our basement waterproofing page explains how to attack the cause.

Checking the Rest of the System

A new pump will not solve a discharge line that freezes or drains back toward the house. Check where the line exits, and make sure it slopes away from the foundation and ends far enough from the wall that the water does not return. In winter, a discharge that ends in a snowbank can freeze closed, so consider a freeze-relief fitting or an extension that ends in a clear area.

Look at the pit itself as well. It should be clean, sit level and have a lid. A pit full of silt shortens a pump life and a lid keeps debris and humidity out. If the pit is cracked or undersized, the new pump will be fighting the old problem, and the quote should include fixing it.

What to Do Before the Installer Arrives

  • ✓Do not stand in standing water near outlets or the pump cord.
  • ✓Turn off power to the basement circuit if water is near the panel and you can reach it dry.
  • ✓Move stored items off the floor and away from the pit.
  • ✓Plug any temporary pump into a GFCI protected outlet, as the National Electrical Code expects in damp basements.
  • ✓Note where the water enters and how high it rises so the installer can size the pump.

The Short Version

Whether you have a pit, how big the pump needs to be, how far the discharge runs and whether you add a backup drive the price. Ask for a line by line written quote and check the discharge route, the pit and the check valve, since those decide whether the new pump works well.
