Slab Leak Repair Options for Nashua, NH Homes

A slab leak is a leak in a water line under a concrete floor. Slab leaks are less common in New Hampshire than in warmer states because most homes have basements, but they happen in slab-on-grade homes, additions, garages and some basement floors. The three repair options are spot repair through the slab, rerouting the line overhead and replacing the line. Which one makes sense depends on where the leak is, how old the pipe is and how much of the line is at risk.

By the Nashua Pro Plumbing team. Published November 13, 2026. Updated November 16, 2026.

Signs of a Slab Leak

Warm floor spot

A hot water line leaking beneath tile or concrete warms the surface.

Sound of running water

You hear water when all fixtures are off.

High water bill

The meter moves when everything is off.

Damp or buckling flooring

Moisture comes up through the slab.

Low pressure

A large leak drops pressure at the fixtures.

Cracks and mildew smell

Long leaks can crack walls and leave a smell.

Find It Before You Fix It

Confirm the leak with a meter test, then locate it. We use acoustic listening and thermal imaging to pinpoint the spot without breaking concrete. A good location means a small opening if you choose spot repair. Our leak detection page covers the method.

Sometimes the leak is not under the slab at all. A wall leak, a toilet that runs silently or a service line leak outside can mimic a slab leak. Confirm before you cut.

Three Repair Options

Rerouting is often the best long-term choice when the line is old or has leaked before, because it ends the problem and avoids cutting concrete again.

Local Note: Why Slabs Are Less Common in Nashua

Most older Nashua homes have basements and crawlspaces, so supply lines are exposed in the basement ceiling where leaks are easy to see and repair. Slab-on-grade construction shows up in additions, garages, some newer homes off Amherst Street and in South Nashua, and in some commercial buildings. Pennichuck reports soft water with a pH above 8, which is gentle on copper compared to aggressive water, but pipe age and installation still decide the risk.

What to Do Right Now

  • ✓Shut off the main water valve if you suspect a large leak.
  • ✓Read the meter with every fixture off and note whether it moves.
  • ✓Dry any wet flooring and protect belongings.
  • ✓Photograph damage for insurance.
  • ✓Call us and describe the signs.

Why Slab Leaks Happen

Pipes under a slab sit in contact with concrete and soil, which can wear on the pipe over decades. Movement in the slab, abrasion where the pipe rubs, corrosion from water chemistry or soil, and manufacturing defects in some older pipe types all contribute. A hot water line expands and contracts, and over time a line that rubs against gravel or concrete can wear through.

Because the leak is hidden, it can run for a long time before anyone notices. By then the soil under the slab may be soaked and the bill may have climbed. That is why a small hiss or a warm spot is worth checking quickly.

Questions to Ask Before You Choose

  • ✓How old is the pipe and what is it made of?
  • ✓Has this line leaked before?
  • ✓Where does the line run, and what is above it?
  • ✓What does each option involve in terms of floor openings and patching?
  • ✓Are there other lines near the leak that look worn?
  • ✓What will it cost to restore the floor finish?

If the pipe is newer and the leak is a one-time failure, spot repair may be fine. If the pipe is old, rerouting is usually the better investment. A single spot repair that is followed by a second leak a year later costs more than rerouting in the first place.

Insurance and Documentation

Coverage for slab leaks varies by policy. Some policies cover the cost of access and restoration but not the repair of the pipe itself, and others cover less. Call your insurer early, describe the problem and ask what documentation they want. Keep the detection report, photos of the damage and receipts. We give you a written account of what we found and where.

Slab Leaks in Garages, Additions and Basements

In a New England house, the places where a slab leak shows up are specific. A garage with a utility sink, a family room addition built on a slab, a basement bathroom with lines cast into the floor and a ranch house on slab-on-grade all carry the risk. If your house has a full basement with exposed ceiling joists, most supply lines are visible and the usual leak is easy to see. Knowing which part of your house sits on a slab helps us focus the search.

If your drain lines run under a slab, a leak in a drain can mimic a supply leak. A camera can check a drain line, and a pressure test confirms a supply line. We choose the test that fits the symptom.

The Short Version

Confirm the leak with a meter test, locate it without breaking concrete, and then choose between spot repair, rerouting and replacement based on the age of the pipe and the history of the line. In many cases, rerouting is the repair that ends the problem for good.
