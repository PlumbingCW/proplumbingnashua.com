Plumber in the Tree Streets, Nashua, NH

The Tree Streets sit in downtown Nashua and take their nickname from the many streets named after trees. It is a dense neighborhood of older houses and apartment buildings, so plumbing questions here often start with who owns the pipe and which unit it serves.

About the Tree Streets

A 2015 Nashua Telegraph report describes the Tree Streets as a roughly 30 block area of downtown Nashua, named for its many tree-named streets. Ash Street and Mulberry Street are two that the report mentions. The neighborhood began to form in the 1800s, when textile mill workers settled near the Nashua and Merrimack rivers.

No source we found draws a fixed boundary, so we do not either. If you are not sure whether your address counts, call and we will talk it through by street name.

What the Housing Looks Like

A 2012 planning study for the neighborhood used 2010 Census figures and 2011 city inspections. It found that most of the neighborhood was built before 1900, with about 30 percent of its roughly 280 single-family homes dating from before 1880 and about 60 percent from 1880 to 1900. Of nearly 500 principal buildings, about 55 percent were single-family and about 40 percent were multi-family.

The same study put owner occupancy near 15 percent, against 59 percent citywide. Those figures are more than a decade old, but they point to the practical reality that many Tree Streets buildings have several households and a landlord.

What We Do Not Assume

Age alone does not tell us what is behind a wall. We do not guess that a house has any particular pipe material. We look, test and explain what we find before recommending work.

Tenants, Landlords and Who Calls

In a multi-family building, the first question is often who is responsible. A tenant with a leaking faucet or a running toilet usually tells the landlord first. A landlord or property manager who wants a plumber looked at can call us directly. Either way, tell us the address, the unit and what you see, and we will say what we can do.

  • ✓Know where the shutoff valve is for your unit and for the whole building.
  • ✓Report slow drains early, since several units may share the same drain line.
  • ✓Take photos of any leak before cleanup, since a landlord or insurer may ask.
  • ✓Do not use drains that are backing up into a basement or lower unit.

Services People Ask About Here

Shared or slow drains are a common reason to call, and drain cleaning is where we usually begin. If a drain backs up more than once, a sewer camera inspection shows what is inside the line. For hidden dampness, leak detection locates the source without opening more wall than needed, and a failed tank is a water heater replacement job.
