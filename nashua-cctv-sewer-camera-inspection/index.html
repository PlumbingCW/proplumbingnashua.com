CCTV Sewer Camera Inspection for Nashua, NH Homes

A CCTV sewer camera inspection feeds a waterproof camera through your sewer line and records what it sees. A clear inspection shows pipe material, joint condition, roots, cracks, offsets and sags, and where each sits along the line. It is the best way to know whether a sewer line is sound before you buy a house or dig up a yard.

By the Nashua Pro Plumbing team. Published November 9, 2026. Updated November 16, 2026.

What the Camera Records

The camera sits on a flexible rod or crawler with lights and a distance counter. The operator pushes it through a cleanout and watches a monitor. The video shows the inside of the pipe and the counter shows how far in each feature sits. A locator on the surface can also mark where a problem sits above ground.

Pipe material

Cast iron, clay, plastic or Orangeburg, a fiber pipe used in some mid century homes.

Joint condition

Open, offset or root-filled joints.

Cracks and breaks

Hairline cracks, full breaks and holes.

Sags

Low spots, called bellies, that hold water and debris.

Scale and grease

Buildup that narrows flow.

Connections

Where branch lines and the city connection meet.

How to Read What You See

A clean pipe shows a smooth, continuous wall with joints that line up. A damaged pipe shows dark cracks, rough scale, loose joints, roots entering at seams and pooled water. Not every flaw is a repair. A few fine roots at a joint may be cleaned. A section of collapsed pipe is a repair.

Pre-Purchase Inspections

Home inspectors typically cannot see inside a buried sewer line, and a sewer line problem is expensive to find after closing. A camera inspection before you buy shows whether the line is in good shape and gives you information to negotiate. Ask the seller for access to the cleanout and book the camera before your inspection period ends.

Our sewer line repair page explains what happens if the camera shows damage.

Local Note: Nashua Sewers and Who Owns What

Nashua operates a municipal sewer system and a wastewater plant. Your building sewer connects to a city main, and the part on your property is generally your responsibility. The City of Nashua plumbing code sets minimum burial depths for building sewers, 48 inches in most cases and 36 inches for septic, with shallower depth allowed for insulated pipe at the Code Official discretion. A camera can sometimes show whether the line sits unusually shallow.

In Hollis, where homes have private septic, the same camera inspects the line from the house to the tank. In Merrimack, the town runs its own wastewater plant.

Sources: NH Employment Security community profiles, City of Nashua plumbing code (ecode360).

Limits of a Camera Inspection

A camera cannot go through a full blockage, so the line must be cleared first. It shows the inside of the pipe, not the soil around it. It cannot predict future roots. A clean inspection is a snapshot. It is still the best tool available.

How to Prepare for the Visit

Locate the cleanout before we arrive. It is a capped pipe, often white or black plastic or cast iron, near the foundation or in the basement. Clear the area around it so we can reach it. If the house has no cleanout, we can usually enter through a toilet or a roof vent, but the cleanout is faster and safer.

  • ✓Tell us the age of the house and any past sewer problems.
  • ✓Mention trees near the line.
  • ✓Tell us if you have seen backups, gurgling or slow drains.
  • ✓Ask your agent for any seller disclosures on the sewer.
  • ✓Be ready to ask questions at the monitor.

What Happens After the Inspection

If the line is sound, you are done, and you now know what is underground. If it needs cleaning, we can clean it and then run the camera again to confirm. If it shows damage, we explain the repair choices and the approximate scope. For some findings, such as a minor offset with good flow, the right answer is to monitor and re-inspect in a year or two.

If you are buying, share the video with your agent. It is a negotiating tool and a baseline. If you are not buying, keep the video on file. A second inspection years later shows whether the line has changed, and that tells you far more than a single snapshot.

Camera Inspection Versus a Cable Pass

A cable machine clears a clog, but it cannot show you why the clog happened. A camera shows the cause. Many repeat clogs trace to a single feature such as a bellied section or a root-filled joint. Without a camera, you keep paying to clear the same line. With one, you can decide whether to clean, jet, line or replace. Our hydro jetting page explains the cleaning side.

Reading the Report We Give You

After the visit we summarize what the camera found: pipe material, total length inspected, the distance and type of each defect and our recommendation. Each defect gets a plain description, such as root intrusion at a joint at a given distance from the cleanout, and a priority. Use the report to compare repair quotes from any contractor, because the distances and descriptions let others check our findings against their own.

If you sell the house later, the report and video show a buyer what the line looked like on the day we inspected it. That can speed up a sale and avoid a surprise at closing.

The Short Version

A camera inspection turns a hidden pipe into something you can see and judge. It costs little next to a surprise repair, it gives you leverage when buying and it tells you whether a repeat clog is a maintenance issue or a damaged line.
